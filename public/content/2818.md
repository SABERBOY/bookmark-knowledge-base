Submitted 20 Oct 2025
The Free Transformer
Abstract
We propose an extension of the decoder Transformer that conditions its generative process on random latent variables which are learned without supervision thanks to a variational procedure. Experimental evaluations show that allowing such a conditioning translates into substantial improvements on downstream tasks.
AI Overview
Picture a toy sequence task. A prompt names a letter. The target is that letter repeated eight times, placed at a random position among randomly scattered exclamation marks. A standard autoregressive model has to settle where the letters go, and how much noise surrounds them, one token at a time, discovering its own structure as it writes. The Free Transformer adds an extra move: before spelling anything out, the model can sample a random latent state that helps organize the sequence, and ordinary next-token generation then fills in the details.
In the paper's synthetic demonstration, reusing a single latent state across several generations keeps the target position consistent across outputs. At a higher allowance for latent information, the noise stays consistent too. At the highest allowance, the model encodes the entire sequence and generation becomes incorrect. This is a controlled toy showing what the latent can carry, not evidence that a language model has learned a useful semantic plan.
How the latent state enters the model
During training, an encoder reads the full sequence and samples a latent state meant to help the decoder reconstruct it. This is the variational autoencoder (VAE) idea: learn an inference distribution while training a generative decoder. Here the encoder's distribution is constrained relative to a prior so it cannot simply transmit the whole training sequence.
The architecture avoids a second full Transformer encoder. The model runs the first half of the causal decoder, then uses those shared representations as keys and values for one added, noncausal encoder block. That block uses a learned constant query rather than queries derived from each input token, which the authors say is meant to encourage capture of sequence-level properties. Its readout produces bit logits, which are sampled and mapped to a one-hot latent vector. A post-sampler linear layer turns that vector into a decoder representation, which is injected into the keys and values at the middle layer, and the decoder then finishes its usual causal processing. At generation time there is no input sequence to encode, so Z is sampled from a uniform prior instead.
Training combines ordinary next-token cross-entropy with a KL-divergence penalty, which measures how far the encoder's latent distribution sits from the prior. A "free bits" threshold κ ignores each token's KL contribution below the threshold and penalizes only the excess. This caps the information passing through Z, but does not guarantee the latent captures any particular human-readable concept. For the large-model benchmark run, κ is set to ½ bit per token.
What the comparison shows, and where it stops
The main evidence compares an 8B-parameter Free Transformer against the corresponding vanilla decoder after 1T training tokens. The Free Transformer used a latent cap of ½ bit per token. The baseline's optimizer hyperparameters and schedule were left unchanged for the Free Transformer, and the encoder adds 3.1% extra compute and parameters. The comparison is therefore not matched in compute.
On MMLU, scored as the macro-average over question subcategories of acc_char (answers chosen by log probabilities normalized by answer length in characters), the last-third training average is 0.567 for the baseline and 0.596 for the Free Transformer, a reported +5.16%.   Averaging over the last third of training is intended to smooth irregular fluctuations in the curves.
The pattern is selective. The authors report gains on HumanEval+, MBPP, GSM8K, MMLU, and CommonsenseQA, while other tasks are near parity or mixed.
The scores measure benchmark accuracy, not inference time, generated tokens, or number of attempts, and the paper reports the 3.1% as overhead in compute and parameters rather than a speedup. The study also does not include an ablation that separates the latent state from the extra encoder, so the gains cannot be attributed to the latent as such. The authors read the boost, obtained without tuning hyperparameters for the new model, as a sign that the method improves the Transformer's inductive bias, while noting that many design choices remain to be investigated.
Relation to earlier latent-variable Transformers
Latent-variable Transformers already exist. OPTIMUS pairs a pretrained BERT encoder with a GPT-2 decoder, extracting a sentence-level latent through a CLS token and injecting it into GPT-2. Fang et al. (2021) use two pretrained GPT-2 models with a noncausal encoder and a pooled latent, aimed at guided generation including long stories. The Free Transformer's distinction is cheaper integration: trained from scratch at 1.5B and 8B scales, it reuses the first half of the decoder as the encoder and injects the latent in the middle. The paper reports no head-to-head benchmark against these systems, so its only empirical comparison is with its own vanilla baseline.
A new place to decide
The synthetic task makes concrete what changes when a model can choose a latent state alongside its tokens, and the large run shows selective benchmark gains under a small stated overhead. What the paper establishes is a new place where structure could be decided, not what that structure means. The authors themselves flag unstable training curves and larger scales as untested.
